_A short post on ‘awareness’ and trying to improve how I communicate to be more inclusive._

A lesson I’ve learned over the past three years building and growing the team at Buffer is how important awareness is to self-improvement. I’m starting to think awareness is the tough part and that most of the battle for learning, growing and improving is being aware of _what_ to improve.

When I joined Buffer, I was surrounded by a team that [values](http://www.slideshare.net/Bufferapp/buffer-culture-06-with-a-change-to-be-a-no-ego-doer) self-improvement and communicating with clarity. Since we’re a remote team of over 80 people across the world, how we come across in written communication is utterly important — especially knowing that there are cultural differences in communication (and many team members have learned English as a second language). You’ll rarely see sarcasm or ambiguity and this is something the entire team helps each other out with to improve on.

Had I not joined Buffer, I probably wouldn’t have recognized how fully important this is — my awareness on communication expanded because of what’s valued by the team-members I’m surrounded by at Buffer. I’m grateful for this.

### ‘Hi Guys!’

In 2014 I wrote an email to the team trying to understand and improve our wording of job listings so that they would be more gender-inclusive. I started the email saying ‘Hi guys!’. This email was featured on [Fast Company article](http://www.fastcompany.com/3044094/strong-female-lead/how-changing-one-word-in-job-descriptions-can-lead-to-more-diverse-candid) and afterwards [Tracy Chou](http://twitter.com/triketora) sent a tweet which opened my eyes to the incredible inconsistency in my approach.

Just as I’ve developed an awareness of how important it is to be clear and remove ambiguity in communication among my team-mates, this was an incredible moment further expanding my awareness on communication. While it seems obvious to me now, I never fully thought about how I worded emails and how my approach could be perceived in terms of inclusivity.

Over the past year since that tweet, I’ve tried to be much more conscious of my use of ‘guys.’ and other potentially non-inclusive language. As a leader, I help make decisions that affect the entire team. Because of that it’s critical that I have an expanded awareness of how I’m perceived and how I act since I represent my team-members — I owe it to them to make this a priority.

I’ve found myself actively focused on making these adjustments as a direct result of that one single tweet:

**Hey Guys → Hey Team/Everyone/Y’all/Friends**

**Hey Dude/Man! → Hey hey <name>!**

I still slip up quite a bit with my wording — especially in ad-hoc verbal communication. It’s one I’m definitely trying to improve on, and if I slip up, I try to recognize it in the moment as I know simply being aware of it is the hard part.

With this awareness, I’m looking for further ways to be able to connect with others in a deep way. It’s the reason why I’ve started to say ‘Hey hey’ as it lets me still connect in a friendly and inclusive way. It allows me to connect in a similar way to what I was trying to achieve when I’d say ‘Hey Dude!’. I definitely recognize I’m only at the beginning of this journey, though I’m determined to expand how I communicate with a lens to hopefully filter out potential biases.

### **Expanded Awareness + Feedback**

I don’t know if I would have ever actively focused on my language with respect to inclusivity without feedback from Tracy or the support from some amazing team-members. Feedback like this, which triggers more awareness is so key to self-improvement.

I believe it’s important to seek out moments and opportunities to expand awareness and learn. This is a key reason why building a diverse team from as many backgrounds as possible is important to me. A diverse team that is comfortable with providing honest feedback will naturally have many more opportunities to increase awareness — which in turn improves the team.

While I’ve been striving to improve my language (among many other things), I know this is a life-long journey. Feedback to increase awareness in how my actions and what I say is perceived is so valuable, and I hope everyone has a safe space to provide that feedback back to me.
